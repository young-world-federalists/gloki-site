# Samira, programme officer (open internet infrastructure and digital-rights funder)

## 1. First 60 seconds

**A.** "The internet promised to bring the world together" is a mood, not a project. The line under it
does tell me what they build, so I keep reading. I'd jump to the status board and look for a team.

**B.** The headline is long, but the second line asks the right question: "is this one real person,
counted once?" That's a problem I can fund. I read on and take the status link.

**C.** "There will be no global democracy without technology." That's a mission statement, and I read a
hundred a month. The next line is concrete, and "this page shows what works today and what does not"
buys it a little more of my time. I would probably still skim.

## 2. Full read: where I got lost, bored, suspicious or convinced

- Convinced (all): the status board. "Today your key works like a password. Anyone who has it can act
  as you." (A/B/C) is the sentence that makes me trust the rest.
- Convinced (C): "We cannot promise yet that this keeps out every fake account." The core promise is
  only "in test", and the page says so.
- Convinced (all): "There will be no binding vote on this network before an independent security
  audit." My foundation offers audits, so this is a direct hook.
- Bored (A/B/C): the crypto-fraud figure. Crypto fraud isn't what GDI fixes. C goes further: "Now many
  people are afraid of AI." That is unsupported padding.
- Suspicious (B): the second table lists Semaphore, ElectionGuard, Tor, Reticulum/LoRa and SMS/USSD. For
  a team of at least two named people, that reads as scope creep unless the page says what is first.
- Suspicious (B): "The code is open for anyone to reuse." Only the node's licence is stated.
- Suspicious (C): "Ouri has offered to write a tool that moves it for you." That is a public promise on a
  third party's behalf, and the claims table flags it as unconfirmed.
- Lost (A/C): "work on the back end with Ouri and Eston". Who is Eston? B gives a surname once, with no role.
- Suspicious (all): "The coalition is no longer active." Why did a seven-organisation coalition stop?
  I need one sentence on that.
- B's length (about 2,800 words) is the opposite of a two-minute read.

## 3. Ranking

1. **B.** It states the gap early (four ways tools decide who counts), shows the research, and is the
   only draft that deals with BrightID, which is the first overlap question I'd ask.
2. **A.** It is short, honest and clear about what is not ready. Its hero is soft, and it gives the least
   technical depth.
3. **C.** Its status table has the best "what that means today" column. But the mission-line hero and the
   long grievance section (crypto, AI) push the problem and approach too far down.

## 4. Findings

1. **Blocker, all drafts.** There is no "who is doing this" section. The only people named are Ouri and
   Eston, with no roles, no team size, no description of how Ouri relates to GDI ("Ouri is part of GDI")
   and no description of how decisions are made. Quotes: "work on the backend with Ouri and Eston" (A, C).
   Fix: a short Team and governance block (names, roles, who decides, who holds the node's copyright).
   This answers the "one person's hobby" worry.
2. **Major, all.** The funder line is a single sentence at the very end: "If you fund work like this,
   please write to hello@..." (A). Fix: keep the contact line, but add a short "What we need" list
   (for example, audit, engineering time, node hosting) and a link to a one-page brief.
3. **Major, all.** The status board is a state list, not a roadmap. Questions the page should answer: what
   is the next milestone; which "Being built" rows close first; what is a rough timeline; what is the
   pilot's target size; what would a grant buy that the current team can't do; who pays for the audit?
4. **Major, B.** "We build it in the open" (hero) and "The code is open for anyone to reuse". Also,
   no repo link (only C has "[LINK: node repository]"). Question: what is the app's licence and where is
   the repo? Node GPL-3.0 is fine. A funder will want the whole funded stack openly licensed, so an
   unstated app licence matters.
5. **Major, all.** The legal home is one line: "GDI is now a project of Young World Federalists, Inc."
   Questions: where is YWF registered; can it receive a grant; what is its link to the team?
6. **Major, B.** The two tables repeat each other. Merge them, or put the protections table behind a link.
7. **Minor, A/B/C.** Crypto-fraud and AI lines (see §2). Cut to one sourced harm figure at most.
8. **Minor, A/B/C.** "Waitlist" is the only user path. Question: how many people are on it and what does
   the pilot test?
9. **Minor, A/C.** Ouri's credit: "The node puts his published research into practice" is good, but
   only B names three papers with co-authors. Keep B's treatment. The co-authors matter to me.

## 5. Keep list

1. "Today your key works like a password. Anyone who has it can act as you." (A/B/C)
2. "There will be no binding vote on this network before an independent security audit."
3. C's status table, including its "What that means today" column and "Read it before you believe anything
   else on this page."
4. B's "How tools decide who counts today" (four options, then "None of them works across borders...").
5. C's "We cannot promise yet that this keeps out every fake account." plus B's BrightID paragraph, shortened.

## 6. Would I act?

**Yes, cautiously: I'd write to the funder address**, which is my only door. I'd ask for a one-pager on
team, legal home, milestones and what the money would unlock. The one thing that would change my answer
to a firm yes is a visible Team and governance block with a roadmap that has dates, plus an open licence
for the app as well as the node. Without those, I'd read this as a thoughtful but two-person research
prototype and wait.
