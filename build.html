How other tools decide who counts

Democracy software has to decide who may take part. Other tools use one of these answers:

  • A list of residents from a city or a government, which only works inside one country.
  • A list from the organiser. The people running the vote decide who is on it.
  • Random selection, as in citizens' assemblies, where a small group takes part and most people do not.
  • Tokens, where people who hold more get more say.
  • No check at all, which is fine for gathering opinions but cannot count one vote per person.
  • Proof-of-person services built on vouching, credentials or biometrics. BrightID is one: it uses vouching to show other apps that a person has one account.

None of them lets people everywhere take part, one vote each, without depending on a government, an organiser or money. Gloki is meant to be that whole foundation: who counts, where the record lives and, later, how votes are counted. The protections that would set it apart are still being built.

The node

Gloki runs on ibc, Ouri Poupko's open-source node (MIT licence). The current version is written in Go. It puts his research on self-sovereign digital agents into practice: software that holds one person's data and acts only for them. Repository and documentation.

A contract runs only among the people who are part of it, and is copied only between them. The design paper calls this subsidiarity. There are no coins and no mining, and there is no single global chain. If you know Solid, it is in that family.

  • Each person is an agent. An agent lives on a node it chooses and acts through it.
  • Rules live in contracts, written in Solidity and run on the EVM inside the node. Every agent that joins a contract is a partner on it and keeps a copy of its state.
  • Partners agree on each write with Cordial Miners, a Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus protocol. It runs separately for each contract, among that contract's partners.
  • You sign with a passkey: a key kept in the security hardware of your phone or computer. The node never holds it. Other nodes accept a write only if your passkey signed it, or if your node signed it under a permission your passkey signed.
  • Apps act through your node. It does without asking only what you allowed, function by function. Everything else goes to an approval page, where you sign.
  • A node is a single program with its own database. You can run it on your own computer, or on a Linux server with HTTPS. GDI runs one public node today.

The vouching rule in the code

Ouri's design picks two existing links in the trust graph at random, which gives four members. All four must approve the newcomer, and the newcomer takes a place between them, so every member keeps exactly four links. Random choice matters: people making fake accounts cannot pick each other. The design comes from Poupko, Shahaf, Shapiro and Talmon, "Building a Sybil-Resilient Digital Community Utilizing Trust-Graph Connectivity", IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking, 2021 (free version on arXiv).

The Gloki beta tests a different version: four people who know you vouch for you. The network does not enforce either version yet. Until we settle on one rule, we make no claim about keeping out fake accounts.

Known gaps

  • Whoever hosts your agent can read its data. Nothing stored on a node is encrypted yet. The host's node can also make, in your name, the calls you allowed an app to make without asking. It can sign nothing else as you.
  • No way back from a lost passkey yet. The plan is recovery through people you trust.
  • Your passkey belongs to your node's web address. There is no separate app for the key yet, so that address must not change.
  • No personal ledger yet. The paper's single record per person, linking what you do across contracts, is not built.
  • Guests trust their hosts. A guest uses a contract through a partner's node, and takes that node's answers as they are.
  • Consensus is unfinished. There is no timeout for slow networks, and how long to wait before a result is final is an open question.
  • No independent security audit.

The node keeps its own full list: what is not implemented yet.

Open problems

Closing these gaps is the core of the work, and it needs more builders than we have. This is where we would start: encryption so that a host stores data without reading it, recovery of a lost passkey, the vouching rule enforced by the network, secret ballots, and an independent audit.

The protections we plan

  • Your phone signs what you do.A passkey made on your device, which the node never holds. In test.
  • Lose your phone, and people you trust can let you back in.They confirm you again, after a waiting period. Planned.
  • Nobody can change records quietly.Signed records copied across the nodes of everyone in a contract. Planned.
  • Servers store your data without reading it.Encryption, blind servers. Planned.
  • Prove you may vote without saying who you are.Semaphore. Planned.
  • Votes counted while still locked.ElectionGuard threshold keys. Planned.
  • Check that the app you run is the code we published.Signed releases, CSP and SRI. Planned.
  • Nodes can hide where they are.Tor onion services. Research.
  • Reaching places without internet.Reticulum over LoRa radio. Research.
  • Text messages for reminders, never for votes.SMS and USSD. Research.

The research

Ouri's papers, all with Ehud Shapiro and colleagues (full list on dblp):

  • Self-Sovereign Digital Agents for a Grassroots Digital Society. ICDCS 2022. What the node implements.
  • Building a Sybil-Resilient Digital Community Utilizing Trust-Graph Connectivity. IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking, 2021. The four-link vouching rule.
  • Sybil-Resilient Coin Minting. arXiv, 2020. Who answers for a fake account that gets in.
  • Cordial Miners. DISC 2023. The consensus protocol the node runs.
